The Night Telegraph Clerk

The rain had not yet started when Arthur Bell came through the station gate at Drayton Junction, but the sky over the down line had the flat, bruised look that told every railwayman in the county a storm was coming, and coming soon. He touched the brim of his cap to old Perkins at the gate, who was already turning up his collar against nothing at all, purely on principle, and crossed the yard with his bag swinging at his side and a whistle half-formed on his lips that he did not quite let out, because Mr. Hargreaves had a way of appearing at the ticket window exactly when a man forgot himself.

Drayton Junction was not a large station, not by the standards of the cities Arthur had trained in, but it sat at the place where the Wealden line — double-tracked, and busy enough to earn it — met the northern branch, a single line of modest traffic that had never justified the expense of a second rail, and that made the junction busier after dark than its size would ever have suggested to a stranger passing through by daylight. Freight came through at all hours. The northbound mail came through shortly after midnight and scarcely slowed unless the signals gave it reason. It was, Arthur had come to think in his eighteen months there, less like a small station and more like a narrow door that half the county's traffic had to pass through single file, and someone had to stand at that door all night and mind it properly.

He let himself into the telegraph office with the key that hung, along with four others of no interest to anyone, on a ring worn smooth in his coat pocket, and set his bag down on the chair he did not intend to sit in for at least six hours, and struck a match for the gas.

The room came up gold around him in the way it always did, familiar as his own hands — the long deal counter beneath the window that looked out onto the down platform, the brass sounder and the Morse key bolted to a board gone dark and shiny with years of elbows resting on it, the block instrument in its case of polished mahogany with its two needles standing at rest, and behind him, the shelf of order forms and the ledger, open already to tonight's blank page, waiting for him to rule the first line and begin.

He liked the room best at this hour, before the shift had properly started, when it was only his and the gaslight's, and the day's paperwork was still someone else's problem stacked tidily on the corner of the counter for him to glance through. He picked up the top sheet out of habit, found nothing in it that concerned him, and set it back exactly as he had found it, because Alice Pryor kept a sharp eye on the order of her counter and would know within a glance if a single paper had been disturbed and left crooked.

As if the thought of her had summoned her, the door from the booking office opened and Alice came through with her ledger under one arm and her bonnet already half-tied, moving with the brisk, cheerful hurry of a woman who has finished her work and intends to be gone the moment the next person arrives to take it from her.

"You're three minutes early," she said, by way of greeting, "which I shall choose to take as devotion to duty rather than eagerness to see the back of me."

"Both, if you'll allow it," Arthur said. "I like to have a proper look at the sky before I commit myself to a night indoors with it, and tonight's sky did not encourage lingering outside."

"No," Alice agreed, coming to stand beside him at the window, where the last grey light was going out of the down line fast enough to watch it happen. "It's been building since four. My father used to say a sky like that owes somebody a proper soaking before it's done, and generally collects the debt before morning."

"Your father sounds a man of firm opinions."

"My father was a signalman for thirty years on the coast line and had opinions about weather the way other men have opinions about their dinner. He'd have told you the wind's backing, too, which means it won't blow itself out quickly. It'll sit and pour." She said this with the flat, unbothered authority of someone reciting a fact she had never once had reason to doubt, and began drawing on her gloves. "You'll have a lively night of it, I expect. The 01:17 freight is running, and there's nothing worse than trying to talk a heavy goods train through a junction with a wet wind trying to carry half your words away before they reach the wire."

"The wire doesn't care about wind," Arthur said, with more confidence than the subject strictly earned him. "It's ourselves the wind troubles, not the electric."

"So you always say. And every time you say it, something happens to prove you only half right." Alice smiled to take the sting from it, tied off her bonnet, and gathered her ledger to her chest. "Do you remember the night in March, when you told me exactly that, word for word, not an hour before the down signal wire snapped clean at the pole by the level crossing?"

"That was the wire itself parting, which has nothing to do with the wind's opinion of me or mine of it. Ropes part. Wires part. It's a different matter from wind troubling a man's concentration, which was my whole point and remains my whole point."

"It was blowing a full gale that night too, as I recall it."

"It is very often blowing a gale in this county, Alice, which is precisely why I decline to credit the wind with every misfortune that happens to occur alongside one." Arthur said this with the open relish of a man enjoying an argument he knew perfectly well he was not going to win, because Alice Pryor had never yet let him have the last word on any subject in the eighteen months he had known her, and he had long since stopped minding it and started, if anything, rather looking forward to the exercise.

"You only argue it because you like arguing it," Alice said, which was true enough that Arthur did not trouble himself to deny it. "My father used to say the same of me, that I'd argue the tide in and out again sooner than concede a point I'd already opened my mouth on. I expect that's why we got on so well, he and I, and why you and I get on tolerably well too, though I'd never say so to your face if I weren't already halfway out the door."

"You've said it now, door or no door."

"I have, and I'll deny it in the morning if you try to hold me to it." She laughed, and gathered her ledger more firmly to her chest, and the moment passed easily into the next, the way easy moments did between two people who had shared a great many ordinary evenings across the same counter and found, somewhere in the accumulation of them, something worth calling a friendship rather than merely a working arrangement. "I'll leave the day book square with everything crossed off. There's a query outstanding from the coal merchant about a delayed truck, but it's nothing that need trouble you before nine tomorrow."

"Go on home before it starts," Arthur said. "I'll manage the wire and the weather both."

"You'll manage the wire," Alice said, pausing at the door with the dry, appraising look of a colleague who has worked alongside a man long enough to have taken his exact measure and found him, on the whole, satisfactory. "The weather generally manages itself, whatever either of us thinks about it. Goodnight, Arthur."

"Goodnight, Alice."

She was gone before the first drop of rain struck the glass, and Arthur heard her quick steps recede across the yard, and then the small clap of the gate, and then nothing but the room and the gas hiss and, somewhere out beyond the down platform, the first low mutter of thunder still some miles off and taking its time about arriving.

He sat, finally, and drew the ledger toward him, and ruled his first line, and dated it, and settled the way a man settles who knows he has a great many hours ahead of him and means to spend the first of them getting everything square before the night has a chance to get ahead of him.

The work of a night telegraph clerk, Arthur had explained once to a cousin visiting from Kent who could not for the life of him understand what a man did with himself for eight hours in a room with a wire and a lamp, was mostly a matter of pattern, and pattern was mostly a matter of knowing what ought to happen before it happened, so that when it did happen you were already halfway to dealing with it. It was not, strictly, only telegraph work — a station the size of Drayton kept no separate signalman for its own home signals once darkness fell, and the block bells and the distant signal lever were as much Arthur's charge through the small hours as the sounder and the key, a doubling of duties Hargreaves had never once apologized for and Arthur had long since stopped minding. The night's traffic ran on a timetable as fixed and familiar to him now as his own name — the 22:40 stopping train from the coast, the 23:15 fish special that never once in his memory had run to time, the northbound mail at half past midnight, and threaded between all the passenger workings, the freight, which ran when the freight was ready to run and not a minute before, and which was therefore the one class of train that could never quite be trusted to arrive when a man expected it, only somewhere near.

Between trains there was the ordinary traffic of the wire itself — messages for the stationmaster, messages for the goods yard, the occasional private telegram that some anxious family member wanted relayed on through the small hours because a birth or a death did not wait politely for office hours, and the endless small housekeeping of the line itself, block bells ringing their short codes back and forth between Drayton and the boxes on either side of it, each ring a question and each answer a certainty, the whole system built, as Arthur understood it and had come in time almost to love it for, on the simple principle that no train might enter a section of line until the section was known, beyond any possibility of doubt, to be empty.

He tested the instruments as he always did at the top of his shift, sending the ordinary call along the wire to Hollow Bank box, where Tom Whitlock would be settling into his own long night a mile and a half up the northern branch, and got back Tom's answer inside ten seconds, quick and certain as a man clearing his throat.

"Evening, young Bell," came the message, tapped out in the loose, easy style long practice at a key gave a man, so that Arthur could nearly hear Tom's voice in the rhythm of it, dry and a little amused, the way it always was. "Storm's coming your way faster than mine, by the look of it. Mind your lamps."

"Storm's welcome to try me," Arthur sent back. "All quiet here. Fish special not through yet, naturally."

"Naturally," came Tom's reply, and then nothing more, which was simply Tom's way of ending a conversation without the courtesy of a goodbye, a habit Arthur had long since stopped finding rude and started finding merely efficient, in the way that most things about Tom Whitlock turned out, on longer acquaintance, to be merely efficient rather than unfriendly.

Ned the porter put his head round the door not long after, shaking rain from his cap though the proper downpour had not yet begun, only a fine misting advance guard of it.

"She's coming on," Ned said, which was as much conversation as Ned generally offered on the subject of weather, being a young man of few words who nonetheless managed to convey a great deal through the flat, settled way he said them. "I've got the down platform lamps trimmed and the up ones'll want doing again before midnight, the wind's guttering them something wicked already."

"Do them again at eleven," Arthur said. "No sense trimming twice for nothing if the wind settles."

"It won't settle," Ned said, with the same flat certainty Alice had shown earlier, as though weather in this part of the county were a subject on which the whole population had reached firm and unanimous agreement without ever needing to discuss it aloud. "But I'll wait till eleven all the same."

He withdrew, and a few minutes after him came Sam, the messenger boy, seventeen and built like a length of good rope, all whipcord and no spare flesh on him anywhere, ducking through the door with the breathless energy of a boy who has run the length of the yard for no better reason than that walking felt too slow for the occasion.

"Mr. Bell, there's a hamper come for the 22:40 that wants loading special, the guard's asking after it."

"Tell the guard it'll be loaded before he needs it loaded, and tell him also that asking twice doesn't make a hamper move any faster than asking once," Arthur said, not looking up from the ledger, in the tone of a man who had delivered this exact instruction perhaps forty times before and expected to deliver it forty times more before the year was out.

Sam grinned, because he had heard the line before too and enjoyed it every time regardless, and vanished back out into the yard at the same dead run he had arrived by, and Arthur permitted himself a small private smile at the boy's back before returning his attention to the wire.

It was, all told, a night that began exactly like a great many nights before it, and Arthur had no real reason, sitting there with the gas burning even and the rain beginning at last to settle into its proper beat on the glass, to think this one would prove any different from the rest.

The stopping train from the coast came through at 22:40 precisely, and Arthur showed the driver the staff itself, sighted and acknowledged as the rule required, and handed him a numbered ticket to carry in its place, since the fish special and the mail were both to follow the same way before anything came back down again. Arthur logged it in, watched its tail lamp diminish up the northern branch toward Hollow Bank, and rang the bell code to Tom to confirm the train had cleared the section, and received back the short two-and-one beats that meant the section was clear again and the line his to offer to the next working whenever it came. The fish special, against all the accumulated evidence of eleven months' prior running, arrived at 23:19, a full four minutes late by its own generous standard, and Arthur noted this with the quiet satisfaction of a man who enjoyed being proved right about small and inconsequential things, showed the guard the staff a second time, handed over a second ticket, and sent it on its way north with a bell code and a private thought that some habits, even bad ones, were at least reliable in their unreliability.

By eleven the rain had settled into the businesslike, unbroken downpour Ned had promised, and the wind had indeed backed further, gusting hard enough now that Arthur could feel it worrying at the window frame in brief shoves, testing the old wood the way a dog tests a fence it has not yet decided whether to trust. He trimmed his own lamp, though it did not strictly need it, simply for something to do with his hands while he waited out the quiet stretch before midnight.

The branch itself, being a single line, ran on a simpler and older safeguard than the block bells alone — the train staff, a foot-long rod engraved with the names of the two boxes it belonged to, which no driver might enter the section without holding, or without holding one of the numbered paper tickets that stood in for it when several trains followed the same direction running. Only one staff existed for the whole length between Drayton and Hollow Bank, and a driver who had it in his hand had, by that plain physical fact, all the authority the rule book could give him to be exactly where he was. The block bells rode alongside the staff rather than in place of it, Arthur had explained to that same cousin from Kent, keeping each box informed of where the other stood so that trains might be properly spaced even though only one of them could ever hold the staff itself. A train could not be sent into the next section until the box ahead had accepted it. Once the train actually entered the section, the box in rear sent the bell code for it, and the box ahead acknowledged the same code back, so that the whole length of railway from one end of the country to the other held together, in the end, on nothing sturdier than a chain of men agreeing to tell each other the truth in a fixed and formal language of bells and a rod of engraved iron. Four beats meant an express was offered. Three beats, then one, meant an ordinary stopping passenger working.

He liked, in the slow hours, to run his thumb along the brass rim of the instrument's case while he waited, feeling the small worn patch where three decades of Drayton clerks before him had rested the same thumb in the same idle habit, and thought there was a real companionship in a night shift shared out across several men in several boxes strung along a length of dark, wet line, each of them alone in his own small lit room and none of them, in any way that mattered, actually alone at all.

The midnight mail going north passed through without incident, a long dark shape hauling itself past the platform with scarcely a slackening of speed, its firebox glow the only warm color in the whole wet night, and this time it was the staff itself Arthur handed up to the fireman rather than a ticket, the last northbound working of the night meaning the last chance to send the true rod along with a train rather than a paper stand-in for it. He logged the mail in, cleared it, and sent Tom the bell code to confirm it, and this time Tom's answer came back with a little more attached to it than the plain two-and-one beats the code required.

"Fireman on that one looked half-drowned going past," Tom's message ran, the taps loose and dry as ever. "Wonder they can see the road at all in this."

"They'll manage," Arthur sent back. "Same as we're managing. Quiet your end?"

"Quiet as the grave, and twice as dull. I've read the same newspaper three times over waiting for something to happen worth the reading."

"Read it a fourth time. Nothing's happening here either."

"That's the trouble with a good night's work in this trade," Tom sent, and Arthur could picture, without needing to see it, the dry set of the old signalman's mouth as he tapped it out, amused at his own small joke in the way of a man who has made much the same joke a hundred times before and finds it no less satisfying for the repetition. "Nobody thanks you for the trains that never gave anyone a moment's trouble."

"I'll settle for the lack of thanks if it means the lack of trouble," Arthur sent, and meant it, and sat back afterward with a small private smile at the exchange, because there was a real comfort in it, in knowing that a mile and a half up a dark and rain-lashed branch line, an old man he liked a great deal was sitting just as alone and just as awake and just as ready to trade a dry word or two across the wire the moment the chance for one arose.

He cleared the mail and sat back to wait, further out on the edge of the night's business, for the 01:17 freight, running up from the yards at Marsh End with eleven wagons of coal and machine parts, the one working of the night that never quite kept to its own timetable and never quite failed to arrive either, sooner or later, somewhere within the hour of its name.

He passed the slack hour before one with the ledger squared away and a cup of tea gone cold at his elbow, listening to the rain and the wind and the occasional distant complaint of thunder working its way east across the county, and thought, as he sometimes did in these quiet stretches, about the strange contentment of the work — that a man could sit alone in a small room with a wire and a lamp and somehow be, in every way that counted, in company with every other man similarly stationed along the whole dark length of line, each of them keeping the same watch, each of them trusting the others to keep it properly, the whole arrangement held together by nothing more solid than habit, discipline, and a few short taps of a brass key.

At nine minutes past one, Hollow Bank offered the freight, and Arthur answered "line clear" and opened the ledger to the proper line, the way he had done a thousand times before without ever once thinking twice about the small formal courtesy of it. The next thing he expected, once the engine had actually passed Tom's box, was the second bell — "train entering section" — and from that moment the section between Hollow Bank and Drayton would be occupied, protected, and fully accounted for until the train arrived under his own signals and he could ring back "train out of section" and close the loop clean.

Half past one came, and the second bell never did.

Arthur did not trouble himself over this immediately. Freight trains ran late as a matter of course, and Tom's own timing of when to send the code depended entirely on when the thing actually passed his box, which depended in turn on whatever delays it had picked up at Marsh End before it ever started, which were no business of Arthur's and rarely worth speculating about. He filled the gap in his own small ways — checked the lamps were burning true through the office window, refreshed his tea, exchanged a few words with Sam, who came through once more on some errand of Ned's and stayed a moment longer than strictly necessary to complain, with the theatrical suffering only the very young could properly manage, about the state of his boots in this weather.

"Bought them new in the spring," Sam said, propping one sodden boot on the fender to inspect it, "and my mother said leather like that would see me through two winters easy, and here's the first proper storm of the first winter and it's already coming through at the seam."

"Your mother," Arthur said, not looking up from the ledger, "was selling you on the boots, not warranting them. There's a difference, and you'll learn it the same way I did, which is one ruined pair at a time."

"And how many pairs did that take you?"

"Three," Arthur admitted, "before I gave up trusting a shopkeeper's word over my own thumbnail pressed into the sole. Press your thumbnail into new leather before you buy it next time. If it gives too easy, it'll let water through inside a month, storm or no storm."

Sam filed this away with the plain seriousness he gave to any piece of practical advice offered him by someone he trusted, and went back out to whatever task Ned had actually sent him on, leaving Arthur alone again with the ledger and the rain and the small unbroken silence of the wire.

By quarter to two, though, Arthur found himself glancing at the block instrument more often than the work in front of him strictly required, and by two o'clock, with the bell still silent and the freight now a full three-quarters of an hour behind even its own generous unofficial schedule, he set his pen down and sat for a moment simply looking at the brass case, as though close attention might coax a message out of it that had not yet troubled to arrive on its own.

He was not, he told himself, worried. Not yet. Freight ran late. Wires got busy elsewhere along the system and messages queued. Storms slowed men down at every stage of a journey, from the yard foreman deciding whether a train was fit to send out at all, to a signalman needing an extra minute to get his own instruments squared before he could attend to the bell. There were a dozen entirely ordinary explanations for forty-five minutes of silence, and Arthur, eighteen months into a trade that rewarded patience over speed in a man's judgment, made himself consider each of them in turn before he allowed himself to consider the less ordinary ones.

He reached for the key at last, not yet with any sense of urgency, only the mild and reasonable curiosity of a man wanting to know what he was actually dealing with, and sent the ordinary call to Hollow Bank.

Nothing came back.

He sent it again, slower this time, in case Tom had simply been occupied elsewhere in the box and missed the first call in the general business of his own night.

Still nothing.

Arthur sat back and looked at the silent instrument with the stillness of a man reorganizing, in the space of a few seconds, his entire understanding of how urgent his evening had just become.

He tried a third time, and this time let the call run longer, tapping it out with the deliberate patience of a man who wanted to be entirely certain he had given the wire every reasonable chance to answer before he allowed himself to believe it would not, and when that too returned nothing but the small electrical hush of an open, listening line with no one troubling to speak into it from the other end, Arthur allowed himself, at last, to consider the possibility that something was properly wrong.

It could be the wire itself, he thought, working through it in the same orderly fashion he applied to any problem the work set him — a branch down somewhere along the mile and a half between here and Hollow Bank, a pole gone over in the wind, the copper grounded out by a fallen limb or simply parted clean by the storm's own violence. That was, on a night like this, easily the likeliest explanation, and the least troubling, because a broken wire meant only that the wire was broken, and told him nothing whatever about where the freight train actually was or what condition the line itself might be in.

It could, less comfortably, be that Tom himself was somehow unable to answer — taken sick, or hurt, though Arthur made himself set that thought aside quickly, as unproductive as it was alarming, since there was nothing to be done about it from a mile and a half away except exactly what he intended to do anyway, which was find out.

And it could, in the version of events Arthur liked least of all to turn over in his mind, be that the freight had already passed Hollow Bank and Tom had simply failed, for whatever reason, to send the code — which would mean the train was already somewhere in the section between the two boxes, running under the belief that the line ahead was properly accounted for, when in fact no one at Drayton had any certainty of it at all.

He did not know which of these it was. That, more than any single possibility on its own, was the thing that put Arthur Bell on his feet and moving, because a telegraph clerk's whole trade, when it came down to its plainest terms, was the business of knowing things for certain, and Arthur did not, at twenty minutes past two on a wet and thundering night, know anything for certain at all.

He went first to the block instrument itself and worked through the small, methodical checks a man learned early in this line of work and never quite stopped performing out of habit even when he expected them to yield nothing — confirmed the battery connections were sound, confirmed his own end of the wire showed no fault that he could find, sent the call twice more at careful intervals in case the fault lay only in timing and not in the wire at all. Nothing changed. The instrument sat as silent and correct in its case as an instrument that has nothing wrong with it and simply has no one answering from the other end.

Then he went looking for Ned, and found him in the lamp room off the down platform, trimming wicks by the light of his own working lantern, and gave him the plain facts without dressing them up as more alarming than they were, because Arthur had learned that a calm man got calm help and a frightened man mostly got other people frightened alongside him, which never once in his experience had made a genuine problem easier to solve.

"The wire to Hollow Bank's gone quiet," he said. "Might be nothing but a branch down. Might be Tom's simply behind with his own work tonight. But the freight's overdue and I've no way of knowing from here whether it's passed him yet or not, and I don't like not knowing on a night like this."

Ned set down the wick trimmer without any special hurry, but Arthur noted the way his attention sharpened all the same, the easy unbothered manner of a moment before replaced by something more careful. "What do you want doing?"

"I want Sam sent up the line toward Hollow Bank, far as the first mile post at least, to see if he can spot the freight's headlamp coming, or find where the wire's down if it is down. And I want you here at the station in case I need a second pair of hands with the signals while he's gone."

"In this?" Ned said, with a glance toward the window, where the rain was coming near sideways now under the force of the wind.

"In this," Arthur said. "I'd not ask if I thought there was a gentler way to find out what we need to know, but there isn't, and the not-knowing troubles me a good deal more than the weather does."

Ned went to fetch Sam without further argument, which was one of the things Arthur had come, over eighteen months, to value most in him — that a plain explanation, once given, was all Ned Carrow ever required before he set to work on whatever followed from it.

Sam took the assignment with the barely-suppressed excitement of a boy handed, for once, a job that felt like an adventure rather than an errand, and Arthur made himself set that excitement aside as a thing to manage rather than share, because a boy running down a dark line in a storm needed clear instructions a great deal more than he needed encouragement.

"Straight up the cinder path alongside the line, not on the rails themselves," Arthur told him, catching his eye and holding it a moment to make certain the words landed properly before the boy's own eagerness carried him out the door. "If you see a headlamp coming toward you before you reach the mile post, you get well clear of the line and you let it pass, and you don't try to be clever about flagging it down, because a freight driver in weather like this has enough to manage without a boy waving a lamp at him from the ballast. If you reach the mile post and see nothing, you look along the wire itself as best you can in the dark — a broken pole, a hanging line, anything out of the ordinary — and then you come straight back and tell me exactly what you saw and exactly where, and nothing more and nothing less. Do you have that?"

"Straight up the path, clear of a coming train, look for the wire at the post, straight back," Sam recited, with a seriousness that told Arthur the boy understood, even through his own excitement, that this was not a moment for showing off how quickly he could run.

"Good lad. Take the storm lantern, not the hand lamp, it'll stand up better to this wind." Arthur paused a moment longer, watching the boy settle his cap. "One more thing. If you find the break and there's a live end of wire lying on wet ground, you don't touch it, storm or no storm, current or no current. You go round it wide and you come and tell me it's there. Do you understand why I'm telling you that twice tonight?"

"Because a boy who's excited forgets the first time," Sam said, with the flicker of a grin despite the gravity Arthur had been trying to instill in him.

"Because a boy who's excited forgets the first time," Arthur agreed, and found he could not entirely keep his own mouth from matching the boy's grin, even now. "And Sam—" he caught him at the door, and softened his voice by exactly the amount that seemed right for the moment, no more. "You're not looking for trouble tonight. You're looking for facts. Bring me facts and you'll have done everything I need of you."

Sam nodded, jammed his cap down harder against the wind, and was gone into the wet dark at the same flat run he did everything else at, the lantern swinging a small yellow circle ahead of him until the rain and the night swallowed it whole within a few dozen yards.

Arthur stood in the doorway a moment longer than he strictly needed to, watching the place where the light had gone, and then turned back into the office to attend to the rest of what the night still required of him regardless of any single freight train's uncertain whereabouts.

Arthur made himself set the freight problem down for a few minutes all the same, to look over the rest of the night's paperwork with a clear head, because a man who let one difficulty consume his whole attention on a night like this was a man liable to let a second difficulty arrive unnoticed behind the first.

Ned had come into the office by then and stood near the window with his arms folded, watching the rain with the stillness of a man keeping himself ready rather than idle.

"How long do we give him?" Ned asked, meaning Sam, and meaning also, though he did not say it outright, how long before the waiting itself became its own kind of problem.

"Twenty minutes each way if he keeps to a walk on the way back, which he will, because he's not fool enough to run flat out on wet cinder in the dark twice in one night," Arthur said. "So the better part of forty minutes before I expect to hear anything worth hearing. In the meantime, I mean to keep trying the wire, on the chance it comes back of its own accord, and I mean to have the down distant signal set to danger the moment we know anything at all that troubles the picture, freight or no freight, simply so nothing else tries to use that section while we're uncertain of it."

"And if the wire's not back by morning?"

"Then it's not back by morning, and we'll have managed without it till then same as we're managing now." Arthur said this with more confidence than he entirely felt, because the truth underneath it was simpler and less comfortable — that he did not yet know what the night would still cost him before it was through, only that he intended to have certainty before he trusted anything to chance on his own word alone.

He worked the key again, patient and even, sending the call out into the silent wire at intervals of a few minutes, more for the discipline of it than from any real expectation of an answer, and between each attempt he found himself doing sums in his head he would rather not have been doing — the freight's likely position if it had left Marsh End on something close to its usual late schedule, the time it would have needed to reach Hollow Bank, the time since that Tom's own code should have arrived, all of it stacking up toward an answer Arthur did not care for, which was that the freight, if it was running at all, was very likely already somewhere inside the very section he could no longer see into.

Ned had settled onto the bench with a mug of his own by then, and it was Ned, of all people, who broke the silence first, in the flat, practical way he had of asking exactly the question that wanted asking and none of the ones that didn't.

"You worked a night like this before? Proper trouble, I mean, not just a late train."

"Once," Arthur said, "in my first posting, before Drayton. A points failure in a snowstorm, which is a different sort of trouble entirely, since at least tonight the rails themselves are sound and it's only the wire giving me grief. That one taught me the value of a stationmaster who trusts his clerks to sort a problem out before he comes downstairs asking why they haven't already sorted it. Hargreaves is the same. He'll want the full account in the morning, written plain, and he won't thank me for waking him at two in the morning to hear half of it secondhand and worried instead of whole and calm."

"And Whitlock? Has he seen worse?"

"Tom's seen everything worth seeing on this stretch of line and most of it twice," Arthur said, and found, saying it, that the thought of Tom sitting a mile and a half off in his own small lit box, working through the same silent wire from his own end, was a steadying one rather than a lonely one. "He was signalman at a junction twice the size of this one before he came to Hollow Bank, some trouble with his knee that put paid to the stairs a big box like that wants a man climbing all shift, and Drayton took him on gladly, because a man who's kept trains apart for thirty years without once losing count is worth a great deal more than his knees are worth complaining about. He told me once — it must have been my second month here, I was still finding my feet with the bell codes and getting them muddled more often than I'd like to admit — that the whole trade came down to one rule, and everything else was only decoration hung around that one rule to make the examination longer than it needed to be."

"What's the rule?"

"Never trust what you hope is true. Only trust what you've been told is true, by someone whose job it is to know, in the proper form the job requires. He said a man who trusts his hopes on this line kills someone eventually, sooner or later, and a man who trusts only the bell and the block and the written order might feel a fool sometimes, waiting on a fact he's nearly certain of anyway, but he never once kills anybody for being careful." Arthur turned his cup slowly on the counter, remembering the flat certainty of Tom's voice saying it, not unkindly, simply as a fact worth handing on to a young clerk who needed it. "I've thought of it a good many times since. I'm thinking of it now, if I'm honest. I hope that freight's simply running late out of Marsh End. But I don't get to act on hope, not in this trade, and neither does Tom, and that's exactly why we're both sat here in the dark not acting on it."

It was not, he reminded himself, the same as knowing the train was in danger. The wire's silence told him only that he did not currently know what he needed to know — not that anything had actually gone wrong on the line itself. But not-knowing was its own kind of danger, and a railway run on it was one mistake away from becoming a very different sort of story than the one Arthur intended his own night to tell.

Sam returned at eleven minutes past three, soaked through and breathing hard despite his best efforts to keep to a walk, with news that was at once a relief and no relief at all.

"Wire's down at the second pole past the mile marker," he reported, the words coming out clipped and businesslike in a fair imitation of the manner Arthur himself used when delivering facts under pressure, though the boy's chest was still heaving with the effort of the walk back. "Snapped clean, looks like, not torn — there's a branch come down across it from the elm just past there, a big one, and the pole itself is leaning bad, near enough over. No sign of the freight anywhere along the path, no headlamp, nothing on the rails that I could see or hear."

"No sign of it at all," Arthur repeated, turning this over. "Not stopped short of the break, not passed it either?"

"Nothing, Mr. Bell. Just the wire down and the rails empty far as the lantern showed me."

This told Arthur a good deal, and none of it entirely comfortable, but at least it told him something certain to work from rather than a wire's blank silence to guess at. The break in the wire explained the loss of the block bell cleanly enough — a branch coming down in this wind was exactly the sort of thing storms did to telegraph lines strung along open country, and required no more sinister explanation than bad luck and worse weather. But it told him nothing about where the freight itself actually was, whether it had already passed the break point before the wire came down, whether it was sitting stopped somewhere further up the section for reasons of its own, or whether — and this was the possibility Arthur liked least — it had not yet left Marsh End at all, in which case the whole of his concern these past two hours had been spent worrying at a problem that did not, as things stood, exist yet.

"Ned," Arthur said, already moving back toward the block instrument though he knew perfectly well it could offer him nothing more than it already had, "I need you to walk up to Hollow Bank yourself and speak to Tom direct, face to face, and find out plainly whether that freight passed his box before the wire went or not. It's the one fact that settles everything else, and I can't get it any way but by someone standing in the same room as him and asking."

"In this weather, a mile and a half, on top of the boy's walk already—"

"I know what I'm asking," Arthur said, and there was enough in his voice now, not panic, but a plain and honest urgency, that Ned did not finish the objection he had started. "But I'd rather ask it of you walking than leave a heavy freight sitting somewhere on a section neither Tom nor I can properly account for, not knowing which of us, if either, actually has charge of it. Take the lantern Sam brought back. Go by the path, same as him, mind the branch at the break. And Ned — if you meet the freight itself anywhere along that path, stopped or moving, you flag it down proper and you tell the driver to hold where he stands till word comes from the station, and nothing he says to the contrary is to move you an inch, because I'd rather explain myself to an annoyed engine driver in the morning than explain something worse to anyone at all."

Ned was already reaching for his coat before Arthur finished speaking, and was out the door inside a minute, leaving Sam dripping steadily onto the office floorboards and looking, for the first time since he had come through the door on his own errand, genuinely tired rather than merely excited.

"Sit down, lad, you've done your part of it," Arthur said, and Sam sank onto the bench by the wall with the boneless relief of a boy who has just discovered how much a body can ask of itself when asked properly. "There's tea in the pot, help yourself, it'll still be warm enough to do some good."

While Sam saw to the tea, Arthur turned his attention back to the paperwork the night still required of him, and it was here, in the gap between one urgent task finished and the next not yet begun, working faster than his usual careful hand out of the sheer accumulated pressure of the last two hours, that he made the mistake.

He was logging the down distant signal's change to danger — a simple enough entry, the kind he had made a hundred times without a second thought — and in reaching for the time to record it, glanced at the clock on the wall rather than the smaller, more reliable one set into the block instrument's own case, which Arthur knew perfectly well ran four minutes ahead of the wall clock and had done since a repair some months back that no one had ever quite got round to correcting properly. He wrote the wall clock's time into the ledger without thinking twice about it, closed the entry, and moved on to the next line before some small persistent unease at the back of his mind made him stop, pen still in hand, and look back at what he had just written.

He sat with it a moment, the cold certainty of the error settling into him the way these things always did — not dramatic, not frightening in itself, only quietly, thoroughly wrong, and wrong in exactly the sort of way that mattered a great deal on a night when every entry in that ledger might later need to be read by someone trying to reconstruct, minute by minute, exactly what had happened and when and in what order, if the night went on to end badly instead of well. A signal logged four minutes wrong was a small thing on an ordinary night. On a night when a stationmaster or an inspector might later need to lay every entry alongside every other entry from every box along the line to work out precisely where a train had been at a given moment, four minutes was the difference between a picture that made sense and one that did not.

Arthur set his pen down, took a breath, and did what the work had taught him to do with any mistake caught in time rather than after — he did not try to disguise it, did not scratch through it hastily and hope no one looked closely, but struck a single clean line through the wrong time, wrote the correct one beside it from the block instrument's clock, and initialed the correction plainly, the way any properly kept ledger required a correction to be made, so that anyone reading it afterward would see exactly what had been wrong and exactly what had been done to put it right, with nothing hidden and nothing disguised.

"Everything all right, Mr. Bell?" Sam asked, watching him from the bench, tea cup forgotten a moment in his hands.

"Everything's fine now," Arthur said, and found, saying it, that it was nearer true than he had expected. "I wrote the wrong time and caught it before it went any further, that's all. It's why we check our own work twice in this trade, lad — not because we expect to get things wrong, but because we know, sooner or later, we will, and the whole business only holds together if a man catches his own mistakes before they have the chance to matter to somebody else."

Sam considered this with the plain gravity of a boy filing away a piece of advice he suspected he would need again someday, and returned to his tea, and Arthur returned to the ledger, moving more carefully now, checking the instrument's clock before every entry rather than trusting his own habit, and found, in the deliberate slowness this forced on him, that his mind settled somewhat too, the small concrete discipline of checking and rechecking giving his worry somewhere useful to spend itself rather than simply circling the same unanswerable questions about where, exactly, an overdue freight train might currently be standing in the dark.

It was the better part of forty minutes before Ned returned, and Arthur spent the whole of that stretch in the suspended state of a man who has done everything he currently can and has nothing left to do but wait for someone else to bring him the fact that will tell him what to do next. He kept the down distant at danger. He drank his own tea, finally, cold as it had gone, simply for something to occupy his hands, and listened to the rain easing very slightly against the window, the storm's worst violence perhaps beginning, at last, to spend itself.

When Ned came back through the door, wet through to the skin and moving with the careful stiffness of a man who has covered three miles at a hard walk in bad weather inside forty minutes and felt every one of them in his legs, he brought with him the one piece of information the whole night had been waiting on.

"She entered the section, near enough on her card, and properly authorized," Ned said, before Arthur had even finished rising from his chair. "Tom put the staff itself into the driver's own hand before ever he sent her on, same as always, so whatever else is true of her, she's not running wild without leave to be there. He sent his bell the moment she passed his box, the better part of two hours since, and swears it went out clean before ever his own end of the wire gave him any trouble. Whether it reached you or died somewhere on the way, he couldn't say, only that he sent it. So she's not lost back toward Marsh End, and she's not sitting idle at his end either — she's somewhere in the section between his box and ours, and has been for longer than either of us likes, and Tom couldn't tell me why any more than I could tell him."

This was, Arthur found, almost exactly the answer he had least wanted and most needed — a plain confirmation that a train was, in fact, somewhere in the section between Drayton and Hollow Bank, unaccounted for since the moment the wire went down, which meant the uncertainty the whole night had been circling around was no longer a question of whether the freight had entered the section at all, but only of where inside it she had come to a stop and why. It left him no closer to knowing the answer to that second question, only wetter, wearier, and a good deal more certain than he had been an hour before of the shape of the problem he now had to solve, with the wire between here and Hollow Bank still down and no way to restore proper block working between the two boxes until it was repaired, which was not a job for tonight, not in this weather, not with a fallen elm branch and a leaning pole both wanting proper daylight and proper tools to set right.

"Then it's her safety we want now, not her leave to be there," Arthur said, more to himself than to Ned, turning the problem over into its next and more practical shape now that the worst of the uncertainty beneath it had been cleared away. "She's got the staff, so no one's going to send a second train in after her by mistake, wire or no wire. What she hasn't got is anyone who knows where she's standing, or why she's stopped, or whether the road ahead of her is fit to be moved over at all. That's a different kind of not-knowing than I've had all night, and it wants a different kind of answer. Someone has to go and be his own pair of eyes where the wire can't be."

"You, I take it," Ned said, though something in his voice suggested he had already half-guessed the answer and was only waiting to hear Arthur confirm it.

"Me," Arthur said, and found, saying it, that he had already decided this some minutes before without quite noticing himself decide it. "I'm the one with the clearest picture of the whole night's traffic, I know the section, and I'd rather trust my own judgment on foot in the dark than send someone less familiar with the road and hope they read it as carefully as I would. You'll mind the office and the instruments while I'm out."

He was already reaching for his own coat and lantern as he said this, and Sam, roused by the change in the room's whole mood from waiting to acting, was on his feet as well, hovering with the useless eagerness of a boy wanting a job and not yet trusted with one large enough to matter.

"I want you at the platform end with a hand signal lamp," Arthur told him, "in case she comes through faster than we expect and I'm not yet back to meet her properly at this end. Show red until you hear otherwise from me or from Ned, and don't let anyone tell you different, not even a driver leaning out his cab shouting that he's in a hurry, because a hurried driver on an unblocked section is exactly the man that rule exists to slow down."

Sam nodded, solemn now in a way the whole night's excitement had not quite managed to make him until this moment, when the responsibility handed to him was, for the first time, entirely and simply his own.

Before he left, Arthur logged his own departure into the section the same way he logged everything else that mattered — the time taken from the block instrument's own clock rather than the wall clock, a small private discipline he permitted himself a brief, grim smile over even in the middle of everything else the night was asking of him. It was not authority he was carrying out into the storm with him, only the plain intention of finding out, and writing down afterward, exactly what there was to find.

He went out, then, into the storm's last hard hour with his lantern held well clear of his body, walking the cinder path at the same measured pace he had told Sam to keep hours before, minding his footing on the wet ballast where it had gone soft and treacherous in low patches, and found the going slower than he liked, the wind still strong enough here in the open to lean a man sideways if he let his attention wander even a step, the rain coming near flat off the fields to his left and finding its way inside his collar no matter how he angled himself against it. He passed the place where the break had been reported and saw it for himself in his own lantern light — the pole canted hard over, the snapped ends of copper wire hanging loose and harmless now, grounded out and dead, and the elm branch dragged half clear of the rails exactly as Ned had left it, close enough to have fouled a fast-moving train but not, as it happened tonight, close enough to have mattered to anyone at all.

He walked on past the break, listening into the rain and the wind for the sound of a heavy freight working hard against a grade — the deep, labored chuff of a locomotive hauling eleven loaded wagons uphill in weather that fought it at every turn — and heard, at last, a little past four o'clock, exactly that sound, low and unmistakable once a man's ear had properly found it, growing no closer, which told him before he ever saw the headlamp that the train was standing, not moving, and had been standing some while by the strain evident even in its idling note.

He found her a few minutes later, standing still on the track with her headlamp burning bright and unmoving through the rain, eleven wagons dark and dripping behind her, and her driver leaning from the cab with the patient irritation of a man who has been sitting still in bad weather for longer than he cared to and has no idea why.

"Marsh End freight?" Arthur called up, though the answer was plain enough from the wagons' loading he could make out even in the poor light.

"Aye, and we've sat here the better part of an hour with nothing telling us why," the driver called back, not unkindly, but with the flat exhaustion of a man who had been driving into weather like this for most of his working night. He held up the staff itself where Arthur could see it in the headlamp's spill, an old habit of proof that needed no asking for. "We came round the bend and found a fallen branch fouling the road ahead, and the fireman saw the wire hanging down beyond it, so we stopped short rather than push on blind. What's the trouble?"

"The trouble's exactly what your fireman thought," Arthur said, climbing up to stand on the footplate step where he could speak without shouting over the rain and the idling engine both. "Wire's down between here and Hollow Bank, and the block's dark with it, so there's no one at either end of the section who can tell you the road's clear once you're moving again — only what a man can see for himself, standing on it. I've been over the ground behind you. The branch is dragged clear of the rail and the pole's down but off to the side of it. You've the staff already, so it's not my leave you want. But I know this stretch better than a stranger would in the dark, and I'd sooner ride the step with you and call the road as we go than leave you feeling your way through it alone. Are you fit to go on?"

The driver looked at him a long moment, rain running off the peak of his own cap, and something in his weathered face eased slightly at the sight of a man arrived with a clear answer instead of another hour of uncertain waiting. "Fit and glad of the company. Climb up properly, then, and mind the step, it's slick as a fish out here."

Arthur climbed up beside him, and the freight moved forward at the driver's own hand on the regulator, slow and careful past the place where the broken pole leaned drunkenly over the line and the fallen branch lay dragged half clear of the rails by whoever had passed this way most recently, and on through the last mile of the affected section with Arthur standing at the driver's shoulder, calling the road as he knew it and watching, in a way no wire or bell code could quite replace, the actual track ahead of them with his own two eyes, until the lights of Drayton's own platform came up out of the rain at last, and Sam's hand lamp swung from red to white the moment the boy made out who was riding the footplate, and the whole business, at last, was properly and visibly done.

"Home ground," the driver said, easing her down toward a stand with the plain relief of a man who has spent two hours not knowing where his own night would end. "I'll not say I'm sorry to see it."

"Nor I," Arthur said, climbing down at last onto solid, accounted-for platform stone, his legs a little unsteady with the release of two hours' held tension all at once. "She's through the section clean, and no one's the worse for it, and that's the whole of what I was after."

He went straight in from the platform, dripping and utterly worn through in the way that only a night's work properly finished could leave a man, satisfied clean to the bone despite the ache in his legs and the chill still working its way out of his soaked coat, and found Ned inside minding the office with the careful attention of a man determined not to be found wanting at the one moment his own judgment might have been called on to matter.

"She's through," Arthur told him. "No harm done, and the wire'll want mending in daylight, but that's tomorrow's trouble and not tonight's. Tom's still holding his own watch at Hollow Bank till word reaches him properly that he needn't, and that'll have to wait on the wire same as everything else, but there's nothing more for either of us to do about it standing here."

"Should I stand down, then?" Ned asked.

"Stand down and get yourself dry, both of you, you've earned it twice over tonight," Arthur said, and meant it fully, and watched them go with a warmth in his chest that had nothing to do with the fire he was only now getting round to building up properly in the office grate.

The rest of the night passed in the gentle anticlimax that followed any properly resolved trouble. Sam had gone home by then, sent off by Ned with strict instruction to sleep straight through to noon and not a minute less, and Ned himself lingered a while longer than his own shift strictly required, drying out by the office grate and saying little, in the companionable, unforced silence of two men who had shared something worth sharing and did not feel any pressing need to talk it over now that it was done.

"You'll want your own bed before long," Arthur said eventually, glancing at the clock — the block instrument's clock, he noted with a private, wry satisfaction, checked now from pure habit rather than any real need.

"So will you," Ned said, but made no move yet to leave, watching the fire instead with the loose, unfocused look of a man letting the last of a long night's tension drain slowly out of him before he trusted himself to walk anywhere at all. He left a little after that, all the same, with a nod that said everything the two of them had no further need to put into words, and Arthur was alone again with the ledger, the ordinary traffic of the small hours resuming its familiar rhythm around him as though nothing at all had troubled it.

He worked back through the book from the beginning of the freight's delay to the moment of her safe arrival at Drayton, writing up a clean and complete account of everything that had happened, in the order it had happened, with the single corrected entry standing plain and unashamed in its place, because a true record, Arthur had come to believe over eighteen months in this trade, was worth a great deal more than a tidy one, and the two were not always, in his experience, quite the same thing.

The sky beyond the down platform had gone from black to the deep grey of very early morning by the time he finished, the rain thinned now to little more than a fine mist and the wind, at last, properly settled, and Arthur sat for a while with his cold tea and his finished ledger and the quiet satisfaction of a man who has done difficult work and done it, as best he could judge, properly, watching the light come up slowly over the wet platforms and the shining rails and the whole ordinary, unremarkable, safely accounted-for length of line stretching away in both directions from the small room where he had spent the night keeping watch over it.

A blackbird started up somewhere beyond the goods yard, tentative at first and then with more conviction, the way birds always seemed to test the morning before committing themselves fully to it, and Arthur found the sound oddly companionable after so many hours of nothing but rain and wire and his own thoughts for company. He went out onto the platform a moment to stretch legs gone stiff from sitting, and stood a while simply looking at the wet rails catching the first grey light, thinking of nothing much beyond the plain, uncomplicated fact that the night was nearly over and he had brought it through clean.

Mr. Hargreaves arrived, as he always did, at ten minutes to six precisely, brushing rain from his coat though the worst of it had long since passed, and made his customary circuit of the station before ever setting foot in the telegraph office — the platforms, the yard, a word with the porter coming on for the morning shift — so that by the time he did come through the door, the day's ordinary business had already visibly resumed around him, and there was nothing in the station's outward appearance to suggest the night had held anything more eventful than its usual quiet traffic.

He found Arthur at the counter with the night's ledger open and squared, the last entry dry, the ink even and unbroken across every line, and Hargreaves drew it toward himself without a word and read it through from the top, the way he always did, in the slow, deliberate silence of a man who trusted his own judgment of a written page a great deal more than he trusted any spoken account of the same events.

Arthur waited through the reading the way he had learned, in eighteen months under Hargreaves, that a man was expected to wait — standing, not fidgeting, saying nothing until asked, because Hargreaves had made it plain early on that a clerk who talked over his own written report was a clerk who did not fully trust the report to speak for itself, and Hargreaves had very little patience for a man who did not trust his own good work.

He read the entry for the freight's delay. He read the note on the wire going down, and Sam's report, and Ned's walk to Hollow Bank and back. He read the corrected time on the distant signal, the single struck line and Arthur's plain initials beside the true entry, and paused there a moment longer than he had paused anywhere else on the page, and Arthur, watching him from across the counter with a tiredness now settling properly into every part of him, found he did not mind the pause at all, because there was nothing in that correction he had any reason to be ashamed of showing a man whose whole trust in him rested on exactly this kind of honesty.

Hargreaves read the account of the walk out to find her, and the staff sighted and the road called by his own eyes, and the safe arrival at Drayton, and closed the ledger at last with the same slow care he had opened it with, and looked up at Arthur over the top of his spectacles with an expression that gave away, as it always did, exactly nothing of what he was about to say.

"Clean work," he said, and that, Arthur understood after eighteen months of learning to read the precise economy of Mr. Hargreaves's approval, was the whole of it, and the whole of it was entirely enough.

"Thank you, sir," Arthur said, and found he meant it a good deal more simply than the words themselves suggested.

Hargreaves was already turning toward the platform, the ledger tucked under one arm as he always carried it on his morning rounds, when he paused and looked back, as though a second thought had only just caught up with the first. "The wire wants mending before tonight's shift, not after it. I'll send word to the linesman myself this morning rather than leave it to find its own way onto somebody's list."

"I'd be glad of that, sir."

"I imagine you would." It was, Arthur thought, very nearly the closest thing to a joke he had ever heard pass Mr. Hargreaves's lips, and he was still turning it over, faintly pleased with himself for having earned it, when the stationmaster went on out to begin the rest of his morning rounds without waiting for any reply.

The morning clerk arrived a few minutes after, a cheerful, easygoing young man named Whitfield whom Arthur liked well enough without ever having troubled to know closely, and the two of them exchanged the brief, practical handover that came at the end of any properly finished shift — the ledger's whereabouts, the one outstanding query from the coal merchant that Alice had flagged the evening before and that neither of them had yet found reason to chase further, a word of warning about the still-broken wire to Hollow Bank and the caution that would need to stand on that section, staff or no staff, until the linesman had it properly repaired.

"Quiet night, otherwise?" Whitfield asked, settling into the chair Arthur had occupied for the better part of nine hours, already reaching for the sounder key with the easy proprietary comfort of a man beginning a shift rather than ending one.

"Quiet enough," Arthur said, which was true in its way, since the whole aim of the night's work, after all, had been to keep it exactly that — quiet, ordinary, nothing worth remarking on to a stranger passing through by daylight, every difficulty met and settled before it ever had the chance to become the kind of trouble a passenger might notice from a carriage window.

He gathered his coat, and his emptied tea cup, and the small ordinary debris of a night's long work, and left the ledger open on the counter for Whitfield's own reference, and went out through the station gate into a morning gone soft and pale and freshly washed, the storm's whole violent passage now nothing more than standing puddles and a clean, rain-scoured smell rising off the wet platforms and the shining rails.

Behind him, through the open office window, the brass sounder began the morning shift's first chatter, ordinary and unremarkable once more. Arthur Bell walked on.
