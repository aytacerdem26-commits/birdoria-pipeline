The Night Bakers of Millbrook

He stood in the doorway of the bakehouse with a candle-lantern in one hand and watched Meg cross the yard from the cottage, her shawl pulled tight against the November chill, her boots too big for her, borrowed from an older brother who had outgrown them and left them behind when he went for a soldier. She was sixteen, and this was her first full night in the bakehouse alone with him, and she walked like a girl trying very hard not to look afraid.

"You're early," Hollis said, which was not quite true. She was exactly on time. But he had learned, in forty years of taking on boys and girls from the village to teach the trade, that the ones worth keeping were always a little early, standing at the door before they were called, and he liked to say it to them so they knew he had noticed.

Meg said nothing, only nodded, and followed him inside.

The bakehouse was a low stone building set apart from the cottage, windowless but for one small pane above the door, built that way on purpose so the heat would stay in and the light of the oven would not wake the village. Inside it smelled already of yesterday's baking, the ghost-smell of scorched flour and cooling crust that never quite left the walls, and beneath that the deeper, older smell of the oven itself, brick and ash and something almost like bread even when no bread was in it. A long trough table ran down the center of the room, scarred pale with forty years of flour worked into its grain. Sacks of flour stood along one wall like sleeping animals. And at the far end, taking up nearly a third of the room, sat the oven — a great brick dome, black-mouthed, cold now, waiting. It had not gone cold in forty years, Hollis was fond of saying, and tonight, for the first time, he meant to let someone else feed the fire.

"First thing," Hollis said, setting the lantern on its hook, "is the fire. Always the fire first. Stone takes four hours to come right, and if you start it late, you'll be baking at noon and selling nothing at the fair, and Mrs. Pardew will tell the whole village her bread came from the Elmscombe baker instead, and that will be the end of us."

Meg smiled at that, the first time she had smiled since crossing the yard, and Hollis was glad of it. He had lost apprentices before — three in as many years, sent by mothers hoping for a trade and taken away again within a fortnight, unable to bear the hours, the heat, the silence. He did not think Meg would be one of those. She had a quickness to her that he liked, a way of watching his hands rather than his face when he spoke, as though the words mattered less than what they were attached to.

He showed her the firebox, the small iron door low on the oven's flank, and how to lay the kindling in a loose lattice so the air could move through it, and how to build the larger wood above that in a slow-rising pyramid, and how much was too much — a mistake every new hand made, piling in more than the flue could draw, and choking the fire before it had properly caught.

"Little and often," he said. "That's the whole of it. Little and often, and patience for the rest."

He had said the same words to every apprentice who had ever stood at that firebox before her, in very nearly the same order, and he was aware of the habit even as he fell into it again now — the small comfort of a lesson worn smooth by long repeating, the way a psalm or a prayer wears smooth in the mouth of a man who has said it every Sunday of his life without ever quite losing the meaning of it. Some lessons, he had come to believe, were worth saying the same way every time, not from want of better words but because the sameness itself was part of what made them true — proof that the trade had not changed, whatever else in a life might.

She laid the first fire with hands that shook only slightly, and when the kindling caught and the small flame stood up orange and eager in the dark mouth of the oven, she let out a breath Hollis had not realized she was holding.

"There," he said. "Now we wait for her to be hungry."

He fed the fire a little more wood, watched the flame settle into a steady low roar somewhere back in the throat of the oven, and latched the small iron door so that only a thread of orange light showed at its seam, throwing a long thin bar across the flagstone floor. The bakehouse, which a quarter hour before had held only the cold blue dark of a November night, began by slow degrees to warm, and Hollis felt it happen the way he always did, a kind of loosening in his own shoulders, an old body settling into a room it had spent forty years learning to trust.

"Why only the one window," Meg asked, tipping her chin at the small pane above the door, "and set so high a body can't see out of it?"

"So the light doesn't wake the lane, and so the wind doesn't find the fire and turn it wild on us. My father built this room with his own hands before I was born, and he built it low and close and dark on purpose. A bakehouse isn't meant to look out at the world. It's meant to keep something in." He said it without any weight to it, a plain fact stated plainly, and went to fetch the sieves.

Meg looked about the room while he was gone, taking its measure the way a new hand always did on a first proper night, now that the fear of the doorway had passed and there was a little room left in her for ordinary curiosity. The walls were whitewashed but gone soft gray-gold with years of flour dust settled into the lime, and the low beams overhead were black with decades of smoke, hung here and there with bunches of dried rosemary, kept for nothing more than the smell of it, though he never once put it in a loaf, and when Meg asked him why he only said that a bakehouse ought to smell of more than one good thing, and left it at that. The trough table itself bore, near its far end, a row of small notches cut into the wood, and when she ran her thumb along them Hollis, returning with the sieves under one arm, told her without being asked that they were his father's marking, one notch a year for every year the ovens had stood, and that he had kept up the custom himself, though he had long since lost count of exactly which notch belonged to which year, and no longer much minded.

There was, he told her, a rhythm to a night like this one, a shape it wanted to take whether a person hurried it or not, and the sooner she learned to feel that shape rather than fight it, the easier the trade would sit on her. Four hours for the stone to heat. An hour, near enough, to sieve flour enough for the day's whole baking. An hour more to mix and knead the first great batch. An hour to let it rise, and another to knock it back and shape it, and a second shorter rise after that, and then the loading, and then the longest wait of all — the baking itself, which could not be hurried by so much as a minute without ruining the bread entire. Six hours, perhaps seven, start to finish, and not one of them skippable.

"It sounds a great deal of waiting," Meg said.

"It is a great deal of waiting," Hollis agreed. "That's the trade. Any fool can mix flour and water. What makes a baker is knowing how to wait on the parts that can't be rushed, and knowing exactly what to do with your hands in the meantime so the waiting doesn't waste you."

He set two wide sieves and two sacks of flour on the trough table, one fine white for the fair loaves and one coarser brown for the household bread that would go out to regular customers in the morning, and he showed her the motion — not a shaking so much as a rolling of the wrist, the sieve tilted just enough that the flour fell through in an even fine rain rather than clumping and choking the mesh.

"Rhythm," he said again. "Everything in this trade is rhythm. You'll find it if you let your mind go quiet and let your hands do the thinking."

For a long while there was no sound in the bakehouse but the whisper of flour through cloth, the small tick and settle of the fire, and somewhere outside, faint through the one high window, the sound of the village asleep — a dog barking once, far off, and going silent; the creak of a gate; nothing more. Millbrook lay in a fold of low hills some dozen miles from the sea, a valley village of stone cottages and a single crooked street, with no history to speak of beyond the ordinary history of every English village — a church older than anyone's memory, a green where the fair would be held, a mill whose wheel had turned for three centuries and would turn tomorrow same as any day. Nothing had ever happened in Millbrook worth writing down, and its people were, on the whole, glad of it.

The lantern threw a low steady light across the trough table, and in it the sieved flour hung a moment in the air after each pass, a fine pale mist catching the flame before settling, so that the whole corner of the room where Meg worked seemed at times to be lightly, gently smoking, and Hollis, who had watched this same small effect on ten thousand nights and never once tired of it, thought there was something almost churchlike in it — dust made luminous, ordinary matter briefly transfigured by nothing more than lamplight and motion, before it settled back to being only flour again.

"Have you brothers and sisters besides the one whose boots you're wearing?" he asked, more to ease the ache in her sieving arm by giving her something else to attend to than from any want of the answer, though he found, once she began telling him, that he wanted the answer after all.

"Four more besides him. I'm the middle of the five. Two older, two younger, and my mother says I'm the only one of the lot with sense enough to be trusted with a trade, which I think is her way of saying the others are more trouble to her than I am, not that I'm cleverer." Meg kept the sieve moving as she spoke, finding, without quite noticing she'd found it, the steady unthinking rhythm Hollis had promised her would come. "The little ones will be at the fair today. They'll want ribbon and gingerbread and to see the tumblers, if the tumblers come this year, and I'll likely spend what I earn on the stall buying it for them before the morning's half done."

"That's no bad way to spend an honest wage," Hollis said. "I spent most of mine the same way, when I was young enough to have people smaller than myself to spend it on." He did not say who those people had been — nephews and nieces, long grown now and scattered same as his son — and Meg did not ask, sensing, correctly, that the sentence had cost him a small private weight to say even lightly, and that pressing further would only make the weight heavier for them both.

Meg sieved until her arm ached and did not say so, and Hollis, watching from the corner of his eye as he worked the leaven forward — an old sour dough, kept alive in a covered crock behind the oven and fed each night with fresh flour and water, a starter that had, by his own reckoning, been alive and working in that bakehouse since before he was born, passed down from his father and his father's father — said nothing about her tiring arm either, because he knew that to notice it aloud was to make it heavier.

"How long has it been going," Meg asked instead, nodding at the crock, "the — what did you call it."

"The barm. Longer than either of us has breath to guess at. My father had it from his father, and he from his, back and back. There's a story that it came over with a Huguenot baker two hundred years gone, driven out of France for his faith and settled here with nothing but his hands and a crock of starter wrapped in wool against the cold. I don't know if it's true. My father swore it was. I choose to believe him, mostly because I like the story better than not having one."

"So every loaf you've ever made—"

"Has a little of every loaf before it, all the way back. Yes." He lifted the cloth from the crock and let her see it, the pale bubbled surface of it, faintly sour on the air, alive in a way that always struck new apprentices as faintly uncanny — a thing with no face and no will that was nonetheless, in its slow, deliberate way, working. "It never dies, so long as someone feeds it. That's the whole trick of bread, if you want the truth of it. Not the flour. Not even the fire. Just something old, kept alive, and fed."

"What happens if it dies? If somebody forgets to feed it?"

"Then you've lost two hundred years in a single careless night, and you start again with a barm bought from a stranger, and it never tastes quite the same, though folk who didn't know the old one won't be able to say why. I've never let it happen. Won't, so long as I've breath to remember it. It's fed twice a day, same as a body, and it's the first thing I check when I wake and the last thing before I sleep, and has been for forty years, and will be, I expect, for as many more as I'm given."

Meg looked at the crock a long moment, and then went back to her sieving, and Hollis thought she understood him better than her sixteen years might have suggested she could — that a thing kept alive by nothing but steady, unglamorous attention was, in its way, the truest kind of inheritance a person could be given, worth more than coin because it could not be spent, only tended.

By one o'clock the flour stood in two great heaps on the scrubbed trough table, fine and pale as new snow, and Hollis judged it was time to mix.

He showed her how to make the well — a crater pushed into the center of the flour heap, deep enough to hold the water without breaking through the walls of it — and how to warm the water to blood heat and no hotter, testing it against the inside of her wrist the way a mother tests milk for an infant, because water too hot would kill the barm outright and water too cold would make it sluggish and slow to rise.

The first mistake of the night came at the water.

Meg drew a jug from the copper pan set warming by the fire before it had cooled enough, too eager to be moving on to the next thing, and had already begun to pour it into the flour well before Hollis caught her wrist, gently but without hesitation.

"Wait."

She froze, jug tilted, a thread of steam still rising from it, and looked at him with an expression that braced itself for scolding.

"Feel it," he said, and guided the back of her free hand near the jug's mouth so she could feel the heat coming off it. "That's near scalding yet. Pour that into the barm and you'll kill it stone dead in a breath, and we'll have wasted a night's flour on a dough that never rises, and you'll be up till noon starting again from nothing. There's no fixing bread once the barm's gone. You start over, or you go without."

Meg set the jug down, and Hollis saw her jaw tighten with the shame of a mistake caught in time rather than after, which is in its way a harder thing to sit with than a mistake that has already done its damage.

"I should have tested it first."

"You should. You didn't. Now you'll remember to next time, and that's worth more to me than if you'd got it right by accident and never learned why it mattered." He said it without unkindness, and meant every word of it — he had long ago stopped believing that a good apprentice was one who never erred, and come instead to believe that a good apprentice was one who erred once and kept the lesson. "Test it again in a quarter hour. Wrist first, always. Your wrist doesn't lie to you the way your eye can."

She waited the quarter hour in silence, watching the jug the way a cat watches a door, and when she tested it again against her own skin and found it milk-warm and gentle, she looked up at him for confirmation before she poured, and he nodded, and she poured, and the water went into the well without incident, and something in her shoulders came down a full inch that had been up since the moment he caught her wrist.

He showed her how to work the barm into the water first, dissolving it, coaxing it, before ever the two were brought together with the flour, and then the mixing itself began — hands plunged into the well, drawing the flour walls inward a little at a time, folding, gathering, until what had been two separate substances, dry and wet, powder and liquid, became one shaggy mass that clung to itself and to nothing else.

"Now," Hollis said, "the kneading. This is the part that will ache you tomorrow, and there's no way round it but through it."

He dusted the table with a fresh scatter of flour and turned the shaggy mass out onto it, and it sat there a moment, dull and unpromising, more like something that had been dropped than something that would ever rise. "You wouldn't credit it, looking at it now, that in an hour's honest working this'll have a life of its own near enough — spring to it, strength to it, the power to hold a whole loaf's shape against its own weight instead of spreading flat as a griddle cake. That's the kneading's whole purpose. You're not just mixing it further. You're building something inside it, strand on strand, though you'll never see the strands themselves, only trust that the working puts them there."

He showed her the motion on his own portion of dough — the heel of the hand pressed down and forward, the dough folded back over itself, turned a quarter, pressed again, a rhythm as steady and unthinking as a heartbeat, and he told her that a man could tell how long another had been in the trade by watching his hands at this work, whether they fought the dough or moved with it, whether the shoulders stayed loose or locked tight with effort that would only tire a body faster for no gain.

Meg's kneading was clumsy at first, too hard in places, too timid in others, the dough sticking to her fingers in wet strings that made her huff with frustration, and Hollis let her struggle a while before he came round the table and stood behind her, his big scarred hands resting lightly over hers, guiding rather than doing, until she found the fold-press-turn of it for herself and her own hands began, tentatively, to remember it without him.

"There," he said again, stepping back. "You have it."

"My arms will hate you by morning."

"They will. And they'll be stronger for it by spring, and you'll knead a batch twice this size without noticing the ache at all, and you'll look back on tonight and laugh at how much it cost you to do a thing that will feel, by then, like breathing."

They worked in silence after that but for the soft wet slap of dough against the table, two rhythms slightly out of time with each other, hers a beat behind his, and Hollis found himself, as he often did at this hour of a baking night, thinking of nothing at all — an emptiness of mind that came with work the body knew better than the head did, a kind of rest that was not quite rest and not quite labor either, and which he had never found anywhere else in his sixty-one years of living.

It was Meg who broke the quiet, somewhere past the half hour, her voice small over the sound of the dough.

"Were you always going to be a baker?"

Hollis did not answer at once. He turned his dough, pressed, folded.

"I don't know that anyone is always going to be anything," he said finally. "I was going to be a great many things, as a boy. A sailor, for one summer, because a man came through Millbrook with tales of the China trade and I thought it the finest life imaginable. A soldier, for a winter after that. But my father needed hands in the bakehouse, and I had them, and one thing led to the next the way it does, and by the time I was your age I found I didn't mind it half so much as I'd feared, and by the time I was five-and-twenty I found I loved it, and never went looking any further."

"Do you ever wish you had? Gone further, I mean."

He thought about this honestly, because she had asked it honestly, and he did not believe in giving children easy answers merely because they were children.

"Some nights," he admitted. "I had a wife — Grace, her name was, gone eleven years now — and she used to say I looked at the road out of the village the way another man looks at a woman he can't have. She wasn't wrong. But I looked at this—" he nodded at the trough, the crock, the sleeping oven, "—the same way, and in the end that was the stronger looking."

"What was she like?"

"Quicker than me in every way that mattered. She used to sit exactly where you're sitting now, on a night like this, and read to me from whatever book she'd borrowed off the vicar's wife, so the waiting wouldn't feel so long. There's a burn scar still on that beam by the door, faint now — she set her candle too close one night, reading aloud, and never noticed till I smelled the scorch. I've never had it planed out."

"I'm sorry," Meg said, quietly.

"Don't be. Eleven years is long enough that the sorry's mostly worn away, and what's left is only glad that I had her at all."

"My son went, you know," he went on, lighter now. "Took the road I never did, out to Bristol, works in an office there, writes to me twice a year and tells me about buildings taller than any church spire in this county. I'm glad of it for him. I don't envy him a day of it for myself."

"Did he not want the bakehouse?"

"He wanted it less than I feared and more than he'd ever admit to me straight out. He worked this trough same as you're working it now, from the age of seven till he was near twenty, and he was competent at it — never gifted, the way you might be, but competent, which is its own kind of thing. But he watched his mother sicken those last two years, and watched me sit up nights with her the same as I sit up nights with this oven, and I think he came to believe a man could only truly tend one thing well in a single life, and he'd rather choose his own than inherit mine. I didn't argue him out of it. Grace wouldn't have wanted me to, and truth told, neither did I, not really. A trade forced onto a reluctant hand never comes to much good. Better an empty stool at my table than a resentful one."

"Do you miss him terribly?"

"Every day I don't think of him at all, and then some small thing turns up that undoes the not-thinking entirely — a letter, or a boy his age passing through with the carrier's cart, or a slant of light through that window there that used to fall across his shoulders exactly so when he was small and working this same spot you're working now. Grief for the living is a stranger business than grief for the dead, I've found. The dead don't write back twice a year to remind you how well they're getting on without you." He said this last with a wry twist that told Meg he did not mean it bitterly, only truly, and she let it sit a moment before either of them spoke again.

Meg was quiet a moment, working her dough.

"My mother wants me to marry Tam Aldous," she said, apropos of nothing Hollis could trace, except that perhaps it was not apropos of nothing at all. "He's got the smithy coming to him. She says it's a good match."

"Is it?"

"He's kind enough. I don't dislike him." She pressed the dough harder than the motion required, and Hollis, watching, understood the sentence for what it was: an evasion dressed as an answer. "I like this better," she said, so quietly he almost missed it under the sound of her own hands.

"The trade, you mean. Or the not-being-decided-for."

She looked up at him, startled, as though he had said something she had not known she meant until he said it.

"Both, I think," she said.

"Then learn it well enough that the deciding, when it comes, is yours to make and not your mother's, nor Tam Aldous's, nor mine. That's the only advice I've got, and it's worth exactly what you paid for it." Hollis said this lightly, but he meant it as seriously as he had meant anything all night, and he saw that Meg heard the seriousness under the lightness, the way she had heard the true shape of her own sentence a moment before he pointed it out to her.

"Does your mother know that's how you think of it? Trade before marrying?"

"My mother knows I've sent you here three nights a week since Michaelmas and hasn't stopped me yet, which is answer enough for now. She's not an unkind woman, only a tired one, with six of us and a husband whose wage doesn't stretch so far as it ought, and a good match looks, from where she stands, like one fewer worry rather than six more years of me smelling of flour and coming home with aching arms for no wage worth the naming. I don't blame her the wanting. I only mean to make sure it isn't the only road shown to me before I'm asked to choose one."

"Then you've already answered your own question better than I could," Hollis said.

Meg smiled, a real smile, and worked her dough a while longer without needing to say anything further, and Hollis let the silence hold, satisfied that the words that mattered had already been said and did not need company.

He turned his attention back to the dough, which by now had gone from a shaggy ragged mass to something smooth and faintly gleaming, elastic under the heel of his hand, springing back slow and sure when he pressed a thumb into it.

"Feel that," he said, and guided her hand to press her own dough the same way. "When it does that — springs back but not too quick, holds the mark a moment before it fills in — that's when it's ready. Not before."

Hers was nearly there. Not quite. She worked another quarter hour in silence, and when she pressed her thumb in again and it behaved as his had, she looked up at him for confirmation with an expression so plainly, nakedly hopeful that Hollis felt something in his chest move that he did not examine too closely.

"That's it," he said. "That's bread, now. Or will be."

They covered the two great bowls of dough with damp cloth and set them near the warmth of the oven's flank, not so near as to bake the outside before the inside had risen, and there was nothing more to be done for an hour but wait.

This was, Hollis had always thought, the hardest lesson to teach a new hand, harder than the kneading, harder even than judging the fire. The waiting. A restless apprentice would find some way to disturb the dough before its time, poking it to see if it had changed, and each poking cost the loaf some of its rise. The trade demanded a kind of trust few others did — faith in a process one could not see and could not hurry, only tend and leave alone.

He pushed open the bakehouse door and they stepped out into the yard for a few minutes, into cold air that came as a shock and a relief both after the closeness of the room, and sat together on the low wall beside the door with bread cut cold from yesterday's loaf and a wedge of cheese, looking up at a sky gone hard and bright with November stars, and ate in the companionable quiet of two people who have worked hard together and are now, for a little while, allowed to simply be tired in the same air.

"Tell me about the fair," Meg said eventually, through a mouthful of bread.

"Michaelmas is passed, so it's the Harvest Fair proper — the last of the year, before the roads go bad for winter. Every stall in the county comes. The Elmscombe baker sets up across the green from ours, has done every year I can remember, and every year we sell out before noon and he's left with half his table still standing at dusk, and every year he swears next year will be different." Hollis allowed himself a small, satisfied smile. "It never is."

"Because of the barm."

"Because of the barm, and forty years of knowing exactly how long that oven holds its heat, and because Millbrook folk have eaten my bread since before some of them were born and won't take kindly to another man's, however fine it might be. Loyalty's worth more than novelty, in a village. That's a lesson too, if you want it."

"Has he never come close? The Elmscombe man."

"Once. Six, seven years back, he brought in some fancy new oven from the town, iron-fired, quicker to heat, cleverer built than mine in every way a man could measure with a rule. Sold out entire by ten that morning, first time in living memory. I thought that might be the end of us." He shook his head, remembering. "But the bread wasn't right. Quick heat makes quick bread, and quick bread doesn't hold the way slow bread does — thinner crust, closer crumb, gone stale by evening where mine's still good the next day. Folk noticed, the way folk always notice in the end, even if they can't say why straight off. He never sold out again after that first morning. Went back to grumbling about my flour prices instead, which is a grumble he's kept up ever since and shows no sign of tiring of."

Meg laughed properly at that, and Hollis found he liked the sound of it in the warm close room, liked it enough that he told her another story after, about the year the rain came through the green in such a flood that every stall but his and the cheese-seller's washed clean off their trestles, and another about the fair before that, when old Mrs. Pardew's goat got loose among the stalls and ate the better part of the vicar's collection plate takings before anyone thought to stop it, and Meg laughed at both, and the cold air cleared the flour-haze from her head, and the tiredness in her arms and shoulders eased a little, sitting still, though Hollis knew it would find her again soon enough once she stood.

"What was your first fair like?" Meg asked. "Standing the stall yourself, I mean, not just watching your father."

Hollis considered this, rubbing his hands together against the chill. "Ten years old, and terrified. My father had the influenza that autumn, bad enough he couldn't leave his bed, and there was no one else to stand the stall but me, alone, with a morning's baking I'd had no hand in making, because he'd risen at midnight the night before, sick as he was, and baked it himself rather than trust the barm to anyone else. I remember standing behind that trestle certain that every soul in Millbrook could see straight through me, that they'd know somehow the baker himself hadn't touched the selling of it and take their custom elsewhere out of pity or doubt. Nobody did. Old Mrs. Aldous — Tam's grandmother, this would be — bought the first loaf off me before I'd even finished setting the row straight, paid double what it was worth, and told me my father had raised a fine boy, and went on her way as though it were nothing at all. I've never forgotten it. Some kindnesses cost a person nothing to give and everything to receive, if you understand my meaning."

"I think I do," Meg said, and Hollis suspected, from the stillness that came over her, that she was thinking of some kindness of her own, given or received, that she was not yet ready to speak of, and he did not ask her to.

"Your father recovered, then," she said instead, "from the influenza."

"He did, that year. Two winters on, he didn't, from a different sickness entirely, and the stall was mine in earnest after that, whether I was ready for it or not, which is how most inheritances arrive, in my experience, whether a man's ready for them or no." He said this without self-pity, an old fact worn smooth by long handling, the way a stone in a riverbed loses its edges. "I was younger than you are now. Frightened worse than that first fair morning, and for longer. But the oven doesn't care how frightened the hands feeding it are, only whether they're fed at all, and mine were, because they had to be, and somewhere in the doing of it the fear wore off and the trade settled into me the way it settles into everyone who stays long enough to let it."

The cold drove them in before long, back to the fire's warmth, and the church clock, faint across the yard, struck the half hour — half two — and the sound of it, thin and true through the stone walls, reached them a beat later than the striking itself, the way it always did on a still night, and Hollis found he had long ago stopped noticing the delay as anything but the ordinary shape of time in this room, one more small rhythm layered over all the others the bakehouse kept — the fire's tick, the dough's slow silent swelling, the clock's faithful half-hours, each moving at its own unhurried pace and none of them answerable to the others. He rose and went to check the dough, lifting the corner of the cloth with the careful, unhurried motion of a man checking on a sleeping child, and found it risen good and full, doubled over in the bowl, pale and taut as a drum skin.

"Now," he said, "we knock it back."

This was, to Meg's evident surprise, a violent-seeming business after the slow rise of the dough — a single firm push of the fist into the center of the risen dough, driving the air out of it in one soft collapsing sigh, the great proud dome falling in on itself in an instant. She flinched the first time she did it, as though she had broken something, and Hollis laughed, not unkindly.

"You haven't ruined it," he said. "You've told it to grow again, only better this time, finer-grained. Bread that only rose once would be all holes and no substance. It's the knocking back that makes it good eating instead of merely large."

They shaped the loaves after that — round cottage loaves for the household trade, long plaited ones for the fair, where a handsome loaf sold as much on its looks as its taste, and Hollis showed her the twist and tuck of the plaiting with more care than he generally gave that skill, because it was fiddly and slow and his hands ached from long practice at rushing through it, but Meg's fingers, younger and quicker than his, took to it faster than he expected, and by the fourth loaf her plaiting was near as neat as his own.

"You've a gift for it," he said, and watched her try and fail to hide how much the words pleased her.

"My grandmother plaited hair the same way, three strands over and under. I never thought it would come in useful for anything but keeping my own out of my eyes."

"Everything comes in useful eventually, in this trade or any other. You'll find that's truer the older you get, though I don't expect you to believe me on it yet."

They worked down the row of boards together, the cottage loaves rounded with a cupped, turning motion of the palm that drew the surface tight and smooth without a seam to be found, the plaited ones stretched and crossed and tucked, and the three small vicarage loaves shaped plainer and neater than the rest, because Mrs. Croft, Hollis said, noticed a crooked loaf the way other women noticed a crooked hem, and would say nothing of it directly but would remember it against him for a twelvemonth. Meg laughed at that, and shaped her three small loaves with exaggerated, comic care, turning each one for his inspection before she set it down, until he swatted her lightly with a floury hand and told her to get on, there were eight more rounds waiting and the second rise wouldn't hold itself back to admire her handiwork.

The shaped loaves went onto boards to prove a second time, shorter now, an hour only, covered again against the draft, and while they waited Hollis turned his attention to the oven.

The fire had burned down by now to a deep bed of embers and ash, the brick above it gone from black to a color Hollis had no better word for than simply hot — a kind of dark glowing that showed itself only at the very edges of the firebox door, a color he had learned to read the way another man might read a clock face. He raked the embers forward and out through the small ash door, sweeping the chamber floor clean with a long-handled broom of wet birch twigs that hissed and steamed against the heat, filling the bakehouse with a smell like a forest after rain.

"How do you know when it's right?" Meg asked, watching him work.

"Old ways, mostly. Throw a pinch of flour onto the floor of the oven — if it blackens in an instant, she's too hot yet, and you'll scorch the crust before the middle's cooked. If it browns slow and even, count of five or six, she's ready. Too cool, and it won't color at all, and you'll have pale sad loaves and a wasted night." He demonstrated, a pinch of flour flung onto the swept brick, and they watched together as it browned, slowly, evenly, over a count that Hollis measured in his head and did not share aloud, some old private rhythm learned so long ago it no longer had words attached to it. "There," he said. "She's hungry."

Meg tried it herself on his instruction, and her count came out close enough to his that he nodded, satisfied, and did not correct her.

The swept oven floor, still faintly steaming where the wet birch had touched it, gave off a heat Meg could feel on her face and hands from three full paces distant, a heat with a presence to it, almost a weight, entirely unlike the gentler warmth of the fire itself, and she understood all at once, standing before it, why Hollis spoke of the stone the way another man might speak of a living thing — because it held, invisibly, the whole four hours of burning that had gone before, and would give that heat back slowly, evenly, long after the fire itself was raked to ash and gone.

"It doesn't look like much now," Hollis said, watching her study it, "swept clean and empty as it is. But there's more baking done by that bare stone in the next hour than was done by any flame you laid at midnight. The fire's only ever the beginning of the trade. The stone's the whole of the rest."

"How long before you knew the count without thinking on it?"

"Years. There's no shortcut I can hand you for that one, no trick of the wrist like the kneading. Some things a body only learns by standing in front of that door often enough, on enough different nights, in enough different weathers, until the knowing settles into you and stops feeling like knowing at all and starts feeling like something closer to instinct. You'll have it too, one day, if you keep at this. Might be a year. Might be five. It comes when it comes."

He set the birch broom aside and crouched a moment before the open firebox, studying the last of the embers the way another man might study a letter, and Meg crouched beside him, matching his stillness without quite meaning to.

"My father used to say the oven had moods, same as a person," Hollis said, not looking round. "Sulkier in a wet season, quicker to temper when the wood's been cut too green. I thought it foolishness when I was a boy — flour and heat and old brick, what moods could it have? But I've stood in front of this door more mornings than I've had hot dinners, and I'll tell you now I think my father wasn't so wrong as I believed him. Not that she's alive, mind, nothing so strange as that. Only that forty years of tending a thing teaches you its habits so well you start to speak of habits as though they were a temper, because it's easier than explaining the truth of it, which is only that you've come to know a thing so thoroughly it feels, at times, like knowing a person."

"Do you think of it that way? As a person, near enough."

"On the coldest mornings, when she's slow to catch and I'm tired and my hands ache worse than usual, I've called her worse things than a person, I'll admit that freely. But on a morning like this one will be, when the bread comes out right and the whole room smells the way it's about to smell before too long yet — then yes, I think of her kindly, the way you'd think kindly of an old friend who's given you forty years of honest work and never once, in all that time, truly let you down."

The loading of the oven was the last hard labor of the night, and the fastest — the peel, a long wooden paddle worn smooth and dark with decades of flour and use, sliding each shaped loaf from board to oven floor in a single practiced motion, the whole batch loaded in the space of a few minutes so that all the bread would share as near as possible the same heat, the same time, the same chance at coming out right. Hollis worked the peel himself for this first proper loading, too much depending on it for a first night's apprentice hand, but he had Meg count the loaves in as they went, and name each one's place in the oven — back left, the plaited ones; center, the great round cottage loaves; front right, three smaller ones for the vicar's house, who took his bread early and paid well for the privilege — so that she would know, when the time came to draw them, exactly where each had baked and for how long.

"Twelve plaited, back left. Eight round, center. Three small, front right," Meg said it back to him like a lesson learned by rote, and Hollis nodded, pleased.

"Say it again in an hour and I'll believe you've truly got it."

The peel itself, Hollis told her as he worked it, had been his father's before him, the long handle worn to a dark shine exactly where a hand would grip it, thinned almost imperceptibly at that one spot from decades of the same grip closing over the same wood, morning after morning, until the tool itself seemed to remember the shape of a baker's hand better than any new-made peel ever could. He had thought, more than once, of having a new one turned by the wheelwright in Elmscombe, whose work was fine and would likely outlast this old one by a further forty years. He had never done it. A new peel would balance the same, would slide the same, would very likely serve every practical purpose the old one served — and yet he could not bring himself to set this one aside while it still had good service left in it, because a tool that has passed through one pair of hands into another carries something a new tool cannot counterfeit, however skillfully made. Meg, watching him work it, thought she understood exactly what he meant, though she could not have put the understanding into words any better than he had.

Then the oven door — a great flat slab of iron, counterweighted on a chain — swung shut, sealing the heat in with the bread, and there was, once again, nothing to do but wait.

This waiting was different from the last. The bakehouse had gone very warm now, the whole small stone room breathing heat from the sealed oven, and outside the one high window the darkness had begun, almost imperceptibly, to soften.

Meg sat on her flour barrel, hands tucked into her sleeves, and Hollis saw that the long night was catching up to her at last — her eyelids heavy, her shoulders soft with a tiredness that had nothing left to prove.

"You did well," he said. "Better than well. Better first night than any apprentice I've had before you, and I'll tell your mother so myself, if it'll help with Tam Aldous."

Meg laughed, a small tired sound, and did not answer directly. "Does it get easier? The waiting, I mean. It felt very long."

"No," Hollis said. "But you'll mind it less, given time."

She considered this with the seriousness of the very young hearing something they suspected might be true for the whole of a life, not merely a night's baking, and Hollis did not press the point further, because he had found that the truths worth keeping were the ones a person came to on their own, turned over a few times, and decided to believe.

"Do you never grow tired of it? Forty years of the same six hours, over and over."

"I thought I would, when I was your age and watching my father do it. I used to think a man doing the same thing every night of his life must be half asleep to it by the hundredth time, let alone the thousandth. But it isn't so, or hasn't been for me. Every batch is a little different — the flour's never quite the same two sacks running, the weather changes how the barm works, the wood burns faster or slower depending on how dry the season's been. You'd think after forty years I'd have seen every trick the trade could play on me. I haven't. She still surprises me, now and again. I think that's why I've never tired of it. A thing that can still surprise you after forty years isn't a thing a man tires of."

"What sort of surprises, this late in it?"

"Small ones, mostly, but real all the same. A frost some winters that changes how quick the barm works, so a batch that should take an hour to rise takes near two, and you learn all over again to read her mood instead of trusting the clock. Or a sack of flour milled a shade finer than usual, and the whole night's bread comes out with a crumb softer than any I've made before or since, and I never manage to find that exact sack again to ask the miller what he did differently. Tonight's been a surprise too, if you want the truth of it — not the bread. You. I've had six apprentices through that door in as many years and buried more hope in half of them than I care to remember. I didn't expect, going in, to end the night glad of the company as much as the help. That's the sort of surprise a man doesn't see coming at sixty-one, and is grateful for all the same."

Meg did not quite know what to say to that, and so said nothing, only nodded once, small and quick, and Hollis, seeing that his honesty had landed somewhere a reply couldn't easily follow, let the silence stand and did not press her toward one.

They sat without talking for a while after that, and it was, Hollis thought, a comfortable silence, the kind that only came after some hours spent working honestly alongside another person, and he found he did not mind it at all that this quiet was shared rather than solitary, a small change in a life that had, since Grace died, held a great deal of solitary quiet and not much of the other kind.

The room had grown very warm now, and very still, the sealed oven ticking faintly to itself as the bread within took its heat, and outside the small high window the last true dark of the night was giving way, degree by imperceptible degree, to something paler and less certain — that particular hour, Hollis had always thought, that belonged to no clock and no name, when a village lay at its quietest and most defenseless, before the first door opened or the first fire beyond this one was lit. He had stood witness to that hour more times than he could count, alone for the great majority of them, and he found, watching Meg's head nod forward and catch itself, forward and catch itself, in the drowsy warmth of the room, that he was glad, in a way he had not expected to be glad of anything so small, not to be witnessing it alone tonight.

"You may sleep a quarter hour, if you like," he said quietly. "The bread doesn't need watching so close as this, not yet."

"I'm not tired," Meg said, and yawned enormously in the very act of saying it, which made Hollis laugh, and made her laugh too, ruefully, at her own transparent lie.

"No, of course not," he said. "Rest your eyes a moment all the same. I'll wake you the instant the smell turns, I promise you that."

She did not mean to sleep, and told him so again, more quietly, as her eyes closed, and within a few minutes her breathing had gone slow and even, her chin dropped toward her chest, and Hollis sat alone in the warm ticking quiet of the bakehouse and watched the window pale by degrees and thought, not for the first time that night, how strange and how good a thing it was to have another breathing creature asleep in this room that had, for so many years now, held only himself and the fire and the old work of waiting.

The smell reached them before the color did — the first faint sweetness of baking crust seeping out around the sealed edges of the oven door, and Meg sat up straighter, nose lifting like a dog catching scent, and Hollis smiled to see it, because he remembered exactly that same lifting of the head from his own first nights in this room, sixty years gone now and the smell still doing precisely the same thing to him that it was doing to her.

"There it is," she said, half a whisper, as though speaking too loud might frighten it off.

"There it is," Hollis agreed. "Best smell there is, and I've smelled a good many things in sixty-one years. Nothing's ever come close."

By the time the smell had thickened into something unmistakably, gloriously bread, the window above the door had gone from gray to the palest wash of early gold, and somewhere beyond the bakehouse walls a cock crowed, once, uncertainly, and then again with more conviction, and the ordinary sounds of Millbrook waking began to gather themselves in the lane outside — a door, a bucket, a voice calling something Hollis couldn't make out and didn't need to.

He judged the time by instinct and by the smell and by the small sounds the loaves themselves made now, a faint ticking crackle as the crusts set and cooled even inside the heat, and when he judged it right he swung the great iron door open and the smell rolled out into the bakehouse entire, filling every corner of it, and Meg made a small involuntary sound of pure delight that Hollis had not heard from an apprentice in longer than he could easily remember.

The peel again, drawing each loaf out in the order Meg had named them going in, and she took each from him as he set it on the cooling racks, her hands moving instinctively to protect the fingers from the heat the way he had shown her without her quite remembering being shown, and one by one the racks filled with bread — round and plaited and small, gold-brown and gleaming, the whole night's work made visible at last and undeniably good.

"Twelve plaited, back left," she murmured, counting them off the peel. "Eight round, center. Three small, front right." She looked up at him. "All of it, and not one lost."

"Not one lost," Hollis agreed, and found, standing there in the golden light with the racks filling around them, that he had no words ready for the fullness in his chest, and so said nothing more, only reached for the next loaf.

The crusts, cooling, made a sound Hollis had never found a proper word for in sixty-one years of listening to it — a fine, papery crackling, high and delicate, like something between a whisper and the settling of old timber, each loaf singing it a little differently, a little out of time with its neighbors, so that the whole rack together made a small chorus of it, faint under the larger warmth of the room. Meg stood still to listen to it once the last loaf was drawn, her head tilted, and Hollis, watching her listen, remembered that he too had once stood exactly so on some morning sixty years gone, hearing that sound for what felt like the first time though it could not truly have been, and finding it, even then, the most reassuring sound he knew.

"It sings," she said, half wondering, as though she had not expected bread to have a voice at all.

"She does. Every batch, every morning, same as this one. My father told me once that a loaf that doesn't sing on cooling hasn't been baked right, whatever it looks like from outside, and I've never once found him wrong on it in forty years of listening close. You'll learn to hear it too, given time, and what it's telling you besides the singing itself. A hollow ring when you knock the base means she's baked through proper. A dull thud means another few minutes were wanted and weren't given. It's a small thing, hardly worth the noticing to most folk who only ever eat the bread and never make it. But it's one more voice this trade has, if you learn to listen for it, and I'd rather teach you every voice it has than leave you knowing only the half of it."

"Look at that," he said instead, more to himself than to her, an old habit of forty years standing before racks exactly like this one at exactly this hour, and finding, still, after all that time, that the sight of it caught something in his chest that never quite wore thin.

Meg didn't answer at first. She was looking at the bread with an expression Hollis recognized very well, because he had worn it himself, ten thousand mornings running, and would likely wear it again tomorrow, and the morning after that, for as long as his hands held out to knead and his legs to stand — the particular, quiet pride of a thing made honestly, in the dark, by hands that had learned to trust a process they could not see and could not hurry.

There was still work in the morning yet, though the hardest of it lay behind them now. The fair loaves went into two wide baskets, cushioned with clean cloth against the jostling of the walk, and Meg carried one basket and Hollis the other as they went out into the lane together, the last of the stars thinning overhead and the sky over the eastern hills gone from gold to something nearer white. The village was properly stirring now — smoke rising thin and straight from a dozen chimneys, doors opening here and there along the lane — and more than one voice called a greeting to Hollis by name and, Meg noticed with a small private pleasure she did not show on her face, began before long to call one to her as well.

On the green, the fair was already half a village in itself — trestles going up in rows, a man testing the give of a bunting line strung between two elms, the cheese-seller from three farms over arranging her wares with the brisk proprietary air of a woman who had held the same corner spot for a decade and meant to hold it for a decade more. Across the way, the Elmscombe baker was setting out his own table, and he lifted a hand to Hollis in a greeting that was courteous and entirely without warmth, and Hollis lifted his own hand back the same way, and neither of them said a word, and Meg understood, without needing to be told, that this small exchange contained the whole of seven years' quiet rivalry inside it.

They set their own trestle at its accustomed spot near the church wall, and laid the plaited loaves out in careful rows, gold and handsome in the strengthening light, and Meg stood back a moment to look at the whole of it — the baskets emptied, the bread arranged, the morning fully arrived around them now with all its ordinary noise and bustle — and felt, for the first time in a night that had asked more patience of her than she had known she possessed, entirely and simply proud.

The first customer of the fair proper was a farmer's wife from beyond the mill whom Hollis did not know well but greeted warmly all the same, and rather than serving her himself he stepped back half a pace and let Meg's hand be the one to take the coin and set the loaf into the woman's basket, and the small ordinary transaction passed without remark from anyone but Meg herself, who felt the weight of that first coin in her palm rather more than its small value could account for. A second customer followed, and a third, the trestle's row of plaited loaves thinning steadily as the green filled and the church bell's earlier ringing settled into memory, and Hollis stood by mostly silent now, content to watch her manage the selling of a thing she had, twelve hours before, not yet known how to make.

"Go on home," Hollis told her, once a second and third customer had come and gone and the row of loaves had begun steadily thinning. "You've done a night's work and more. Sleep first. The trade will still be here when you wake, same as the bread was there this morning."

Meg did not argue. Her legs had gone heavy the way legs do only after a night spent entirely on them, and she left him the empty baskets to gather and turned back up the lane the way they'd come.

Hollis watched her go from behind his trestle, a small figure growing smaller in the low gold light, flour still pale on the hem of her skirt, and found himself thinking that the bakehouse had stood windowless and close for forty years because his father had built it to keep something in, and that he had spent most of those forty years believing the something was only heat, only bread, only the fire itself. He was no longer entirely certain that was the whole of it. For the first time in eleven long years, he found he was no longer the only one left to tend it.
